## What Is FDA Import Compliance?

FDA import compliance is the set of U.S. Food and Drug Administration requirements that apply to food, drugs, cosmetics, dietary supplements, medical devices, and tobacco products entering the United States — including those manufactured in China. Unlike a single certification mark (such as CE or FCC), FDA compliance is a process: the foreign facility must be registered, each food shipment requires prior notice, the product must meet substance and labeling standards, and the importer must keep records for FDA inspection. For most consumer-goods importers from China, the relevant FDA centers are **CFSAN** (Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition) for food and food-contact materials, and **CDER**/**CDRH** for drugs and devices. Enforcement happens at U.S. ports of entry through **detention without physical examination** (DWPE) and physical sampling.

## FDA-Regulated Product Categories from China

Not every product imported from China falls under FDA — but if it touches food, the body, or claims a health benefit, it likely does. Here is how the main categories map to FDA requirements.

| | Product Category || FDA Center || Key Requirement || Documentation |

| **Food (human & animal)** || CFSAN || Facility registration + prior notice + FSMA preventive controls || FFR number, PN confirmation, supplier audit |

| **Food-contact materials** || CFSAN || 21 CFR 174–189 migration compliance || Migration test report, FCM declaration |

| **Cosmetics** || CDER (OVC) || MoCRA registration, adverse-event reporting, U.S. RP || CPIS registration, SRP name, ingredient list |

| **Dietary supplements** || CDER || cGMP 21 CFR 111, DSHEA labeling || cGMP audit, certificate of analysis, label review |

| **Medical devices** || CDRH || 510(k) or PMA, establishment registration || 510(k) clearance, device listing |

| **Drugs (OTC & Rx)** || CDER || NDANDA, GMP, drug listing || NDA/ANDA approval, DMF |

## Food Facility Registration & Prior Notice

For food imports, the two non-negotiable administrative steps are facility registration and prior notice filing.

 - **Food Facility Registration (FFR)** — Every foreign facility that manufactures, processes, packs, or holds food for consumption in the U.S. must register with FDA (21 CFR 1, Subpart H). Registration is free and must be renewed every even-numbered year. The **U.S. Agent** must be a resident of the United States.
 - **Prior Notice (PN)** — For every food shipment, the importer, broker, or carrier must file prior notice with FDA before the food arrives in the U.S. The minimum filing windows depend on the mode of transport.

| | Mode of Transport || Minimum Prior Notice Before Arrival |

| By air || **2 hours** before arrival |

| By rail || **4 hours** before arrival |

| By sea || **8 hours** before arrival |

| By road (truck) || **2 hours** before arrival |

| By mail/international mail || At time of mailing |

Both FFR and PN are submitted through the **FDA PNS (Prior Notice System Interface)** or via **ACE** (Automated Commercial Environment) used by customs brokers. A missing or late PN results in **FDA refusal** and the shipment being held or returned.

## Food-Contact Materials: 21 CFR 174–189 Migration Tests

Products intended to come into contact with food (silicone spatulas, stainless steel water bottles, melamine plates, bamboo bowls, non-stick pans) must comply with **21 CFR Parts 174–189**, which set specific migration limits (SML) and overall migration limits (OML) for substances that may transfer from the material into food.

| | Material || 21 CFR Reference || Test Simulant || Limit |

| Silicone rubber || 21 CFR 177.2600 || Distilled water, n-heptane || Residue ≤ 0.5 mg/in² |

| Melamine-formaldehyde || 21 CFR 177.1460 || Water, 3% acetic acid, 8% ethanol || Melamine within SML |

| Stainless steel || 21 CFR 175.300 / 178.3800 || 4% acetic acid (boiling) || Cr, Ni, Pb within limits |

| Polyethylene (PE) || 21 CFR 177.1520 || Water, n-heptane || Extractives ≤ limits |

| Non-stick coating (PTFE) || 21 CFR 177.1550 || Distilled water, n-heptane || ≤ 0.020 mg/in² |

| Bamboo / wood || 21 CFR 178.3800 || Water, 8% ethanol || Extractives limit |

Migration tests are run by FDA-recognized or ISO 17025-accredited labs (SGS, Intertek, Bureau Veritas, Eurofins) in China. A single-material migration test costs **$300–$800**; a full kitchenware set with multiple materials can run **$1,200–$2,500** and take **7–14 business days**.

## Worked Example: Silicone Food Container Fails Migration Test

A U.S. Amazon seller imports **8,000 silicone food-storage containers** (set of 3, $1.80 EXW each) from a Shenzhen factory. The factory claims "FDA-grade silicone" but provides no test report. The seller runs a 21 CFR 177.2600 migration test at SGS Shenzhen before mass shipment.

**Test result:** n-heptane extractables measured at **0.82 mg/in²** — above the 21 CFR 177.2600 limit of **0.5 mg/in²**.

 - Root cause: factory used a low-cost silicone with excess plasticizer and filler
 - Migration test cost: **$650** for 2 simulants, 7-day turnaround
 - Reformulation: switch to **food-grade LSR (liquid silicone rubber)** with FDA-compliant curing agent
 - Reformulation cost increase: **+$0.22/unit** = **$1,760** for 8,000 units
 - Re-test cost: **$650**
 - Without the test: FDA detention at LAX, **$250–$500/day** storage, plus Amazon listing suspension and potential recall

The seller approved the reformulation, re-tested (passed at 0.21 mg/in²), and shipped. Total added compliance cost: **$2,410** — far less than the estimated **$30,000+** in detention, storage, and lost Amazon rank. ## Top FDA Detention Reasons for Chinese Imports

FDA publishes **Import Alerts** naming products and countries with a history of violations. China is frequently listed for the following categories, which trigger **detention without physical examination (DWPE)**:

| | Detention Reason || Typical Product || FDA Basis |

| **Salmonella / microbial contamination** || Dried seafood, spices, herbal tea || 21 CFR 110 (GMP); adulterated food |

| **Pesticide residue** || Frozen vegetables, fruit, tea || Tolerance violations (40 CFR 180) |

| **Filth (insects, rodent, mold)** || Nuts, grains, dried fruit || Defect action levels (DAL) |

| **Labeling violations** || All food categories || Missing allergen, wrong nutrition facts |

| **Unauthorized additives** || Food color, preservatives || 21 CFR 70–80 (food additives) |

| **Melamine migration** || Melamine tableware || 21 CFR 177.1460 |

Labeling is the #1 avoidable rejection. Under the **FASTER Act of 2021**, sesame became the 9th major food allergen and must be declared on the label. Misbranded food is refused even if the product itself is safe.

## Cosmetics, Supplements & Devices: What You Actually Need

For non-food FDA-regulated products, the requirements differ significantly.

 - **Cosmetics (MoCRA 2022)** — Since December 2023, cosmetic facilities must register in **CPIS** and each product must have a **U.S. Responsible Person (SRP)**. Ingredient labeling and adverse-event reporting are mandatory. No pre-approval is needed, but products containing prohibited ingredients (e.g. mercury in skin lighteners) or restricted color additives are refused.
 - **Dietary supplements (DSHEA + cGMP)** — Must be manufactured under **21 CFR 111 (cGMP)**, labeled with a Supplement Facts panel, and must not contain unapproved new dietary ingredients (NDI). The importer must keep a **Certificate of Analysis (CoA)** for each lot.
 - **Medical devices** — Class I devices can be listed; Class II typically require **510(k) clearance**; Class III require **PMA**. Foreign manufacturers need a **U.S. Agent** and establishment registration.

For most Yiwu-sourced consumer goods (kitchenware, skincare, vitamins), the practical path is: confirm the category, get the right test or audit, and keep records for 2 years (food) or longer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Do I need FDA registration to import food from China?

Yes. Any foreign facility that manufactures, processes, packs, or holds food for U.S. consumption must hold a valid **FDA Food Facility Registration (FFR)**. Registration is free, filed online, and must be renewed every even-numbered year (2024, 2026, etc.). You must also name a **U.S. Agent** who resides in the United States.

### 2. What is prior notice and when must I file it?

**Prior Notice (PN)** is a notification to FDA that food is being imported into the U.S. It must be filed before the food arrives: at least **2 hours** before arrival by air or truck, **4 hours** by rail, and **8 hours** by sea. PN is free and submitted through the FDA PNS or ACE system. Late or missing PN causes the shipment to be refused.

### 3. What 21 CFR rules apply to food-contact materials?

Food-contact materials are regulated under **21 CFR Parts 174–189**. Part 174 sets general principles, Part 175 covers adhesives and coatings, Part 176 covers paper and paperboard, Part 177 covers polymers (silicone 177.2600, polyethylene 177.1520, melamine 177.1460), and Part 178 covers additives. Each part sets specific and overall migration limits measured in food simulants (water, 3% acetic acid, 8%/50% ethanol, n-heptane).

### 4. What are the most common FDA detention reasons for Chinese imports?

The top reasons are **microbial contamination** (Salmonella, Listeria, E. coli), **pesticide residue** exceeding tolerances, **filth** (insects, mold, rodent), **labeling failures** (missing allergen declarations like sesame, incorrect nutrition facts), and **unauthorized food additives**. FDA Import Alerts on Chinese products can trigger detention without physical examination.

### 5. Do cosmetics need FDA approval before import?

Cosmetics do **not** require pre-market approval, but since the **Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act (MoCRA, 2022)**, cosmetic facilities must register in FDA's **CPIS**, each product needs a **U.S. Responsible Person**, and ingredient labeling plus adverse-event reporting are mandatory. Prohibited ingredients (e.g. certain mercury compounds) and restricted color additives will trigger refusal.

### 6. How much does FDA food-contact testing cost in China?

A single-material migration test (e.g. silicone against 21 CFR 177.2600) costs **$300–$800** at an ISO 17025 lab (SGS, Intertek, BV, Eurofins) and takes **7–14 business days**. A full kitchenware set with multiple materials and simulants runs **$1,200–$2,500**. Registration (FFR) and prior notice are free of charge.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Using the supplier's test report without verification.** Many factories provide fake or expired CE/FCC reports. Verify the report number on the certification body's website before placing a bulk order.
- **Applying the wrong certification for the destination market.** CE for EU, FCC/CPC for US, UKCA for UK, RCM for Australia. Shipping CE-marked goods to the US = customs seizure and $10k+ fines.
- **No test report for the exact product variant.** A CE report for a red 220V version does not cover a blue 110V version. Each variant needs its own test or a report covering all variants.
- **Forgetting ongoing compliance.** Regulations change (e.g., EU GPSR replacing GPSD, US CPSC updates). A cert valid today may be invalid in 12 months. Track renewal dates and budget for re-testing.
- **Under-declaring product category to skip certification.** Listing a children's toy as 'home decoration' to avoid CPC is customs fraud. The buyer of record (you) carries the liability.
