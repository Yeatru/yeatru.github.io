Supplier Corrective Action Request (SCAR) for Chinese Factories: Template & Process

The 8-step SCAR/CAPA process, 5 Whys and fishbone root-cause analysis, a required-fields template, and a 4.2% → 0.6% case study.

Key Takeaways

  • A SCAR (Supplier Corrective Action Request) fixes the factory; a rework only fixes one shipment. If the same defect hits 2–3 batches, stop reworking and open a SCAR.
  • The 8-step process runs problem statement → containment → root cause (5 Whys / fishbone) → corrective action → verification → standardization → closure → tracking.
  • A root cause that names a person ("operator careless") is not a root cause; real ones point to a process, tool, material or measurement gap — see defect classification for framing the defect.
  • Field the template with 11 required fields, including a preventive action, an owner and a due date per action, and a verification method.
  • A working SCAR cut one factory's recurring defect from 4.2% to 0.6% across 3 verified batches, and it feeds directly into supplier tiering.

What Is a Supplier Corrective Action Request (SCAR)?

A Supplier Corrective Action Request (SCAR) is a formal, documented request that a supplier investigate the root cause of a defect and change its process so the defect cannot recur. It sits inside the wider CAPA system (Corrective and Preventive Action): the corrective action fixes the specific batch, and the preventive action changes the process permanently. A SCAR is triggered when a defect is critical, when a defect rate breaches your AQL 2.5 major standard, or when the same defect reappears across batches — precisely when a one-off rework has already proven insufficient.

SCAR vs CAPA vs One-Off Rework

Buyers confuse three different tools. They are not interchangeable, and using the wrong one is why the same defect keeps coming back.

Tool Scope Output Use When
One-off rework This batch only Fixed units, same process A single, isolated defect that will not recur
CAPA (system) Supplier quality system Documented corrective + preventive process Ongoing quality management with a supplier you keep
SCAR (document) A single, specific defect event Root cause + actions + verification + close-out Critical defect, AQL breach, or a defect recurring 2–3 times

Put simply: rework fixes the shipment, a SCAR fixes the factory. A CAPA is the framework; a SCAR is the document you file inside it for each serious event.

The 8-Step SCAR Process

  1. Problem description. State the defect, part/SKU and drawing revision, the batch, the quantity found, the detection point and the date. "Guide marks 0.4 mm out of tolerance on 6 of 50 units, batch B-0912, found at PSI on 12 Oct" is a problem statement; "quality is poor" is not.
  2. Immediate containment. Stop the line or quarantine stock within 24–48 hours, segregate suspect batches, and run a 100% sort or re-inspection on already-packed units. Containment protects this shipment while the real fix is developed.
  3. Root cause analysis. Use 5 Whys or a fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram across man, machine, material, method, measurement and environment. The factory, not the buyer, must own this analysis — see the section below.
  4. Corrective action. The action that fixes the root cause for the specific batch: a tooling adjustment, a material spec change, a re-trained station. Needs an owner and a due date.
  5. Preventive action. The action that stops recurrence across all future batches: a jig, an added in-line inspection point, an updated work instruction or control plan. Without this field a SCAR fails.
  6. Verification. Run the next 3 batches at the new process and measure the defect rate against the target (for example clear the AQL 2.5 major limit by a wide margin).
  7. Standardization. Lock the change into the work instruction, control plan and golden sample; update the supplier scorecard to reflect the improvement.
  8. Closure and tracking. Close the SCAR in writing with the verification evidence attached, and track the defect trend for at least one more quarter to confirm it holds.

Root Cause Analysis: 5 Whys and the Fishbone

The root cause section is where most Chinese factories fail. A weak reply stops at the first plausible answer — usually "operator error". Push the analysis until it lands on something the factory can change structurally. A worked 5 Whys for the guide-mark defect:

  • Why did the guide marks land out of tolerance? The print plate shifted during the run.
  • Why did the plate shift? The plate-lock bolt was not torqued.
  • Why was it not torqued? The torque wrench at the station was out of calibration.
  • Why was the wrench out of calibration? There is no calibration schedule for station tools.
  • Why is there no calibration schedule? The control plan never listed a calibration interval for this station. → Root cause: a missing control-plan requirement, not a careless worker.

The matching fishbone covers six branches — man, machine, material, method, measurement, environment. Add the calibration interval to the control plan (preventive action), re-torque and re-verify the affected plate (corrective action), and verify across 3 batches.

The SCAR Template: Required Fields

Use this 11-field template so every SCAR is complete and auditable. A SCAR missing the preventive action or the owner will not stop recurrence.

# Field What to Capture
1 SCAR number & date Unique ID, issue date, revision
2 Supplier & contact Factory name, buyer PO, named quality contact
3 Part / SKU & drawing rev Product, drawing revision, golden-sample ID
4 Problem statement Defect, quantity, batch, detection point, date
5 Immediate containment Sort/quarantine action, units affected, date
6 Root cause 5 Whys or fishbone result, systemic not personal
7 Corrective action Fix for the specific batch, owner, due date
8 Preventive action Process change to stop recurrence, owner, due date
9 Verification method 3-batch check, measurement, target defect rate
10 Effectiveness result Measured defect rate per batch after the fix
11 Close-out sign-off Buyer + supplier signatures, date, evidence attached

The 6 Signs of a "Wrong Problem" or a Deflecting Reply

You will know a SCAR response is hollow before you finish reading it. Screen for these six:

  1. Blaming the worker. "Operator carelessness" is an outcome, not a root cause.
  2. Blaming the buyer. "Your spec is unclear" when the drawing was signed and sampled.
  3. "We will strengthen inspection" as the only action. Inspecting harder does not fix a process that produces defects.
  4. No preventive action. A corrective action alone means the defect returns on the next run.
  5. No owner and no date. A vague "will improve quality soon" cannot be verified or enforced.
  6. No measurement. No defect rate, no batch data, no test — nothing you can check against the target.

If any two of these appear, return the SCAR unclosed and require a re-submission within 7 days, with the balance held until it passes.

Case Study: 4.2% → 0.6% Across 3 Batches

A European brand sourced 18,000 silicone hose clamps per year at $3.20/unit (≈$57,600 annual spend), MOQ 3,000 from a Ningbo factory. For three consecutive batches the same defect appeared: a cracked plastic lever at a 4.2% rate — breaching the AQL 2.5 major limit every time and generating returns. Each batch had been "reworked" and each batch failed again. We opened a SCAR.

SCAR Stage Action Taken Timeline Result
Problem & containment 100% sort of 3,000 units; line stopped Day 1–2 Defective batch isolated
Root cause (5 Whys) Reached a missing gate-cooling time in the control plan Day 7 Systemic cause identified
Corrective + preventive Added cooling time + mold-temperature check to control plan Day 14–21 Process changed, tooling verified
Verification Ran 3 batches, AQL 2.5 major sampling each Day 30–60 Defect 4.2% → 0.6%
Standardization & closure Work instruction and scorecard updated, SCAR signed Day 60 Trend held for 2 quarters

The defect rate fell from 4.2% to 0.6% — a 3.6-point improvement on a $57,600 annual spend, removing roughly $2,076/year in defects plus returns and rework labour. Verification cost $240 (3 batches × ~$80/man-day), so the SCAR paid for itself almost 9× in the first year, and it moved the factory up a tier in the supplier scorecard.

One-Off Rework vs Systematic CAPA

Dimension One-Off Rework Systematic CAPA / SCAR
Fixes One batch The process, permanently
Root cause Not investigated 5 Whys / fishbone documented
Recurrence Likely — same defect returns Prevented by preventive action
Cost Labour per batch, every batch One-time verification ($80–$300/man-day)
Evidence None retained Auditable SCAR record
Supplier score Unchanged Tier and rating updated

Linking SCAR to Supplier Tiering & SRM

A closed SCAR is not just a quality record — it is supplier-relationship-management data. Add an "open SCARs" and "SCAR close-out time" column to your supplier scorecard. A factory that closes SCARs on time and demonstrably lowers its defect rate earns volume and moves up a tier; one that misses deadlines twice, cannot verify a root cause, or lets a defect recur after closure should be down-tiered and given a 30–60 day improvement plan with a small pilot PO before any further volume. This is how SCAR discipline connects to supplier relationship management and to your sourcing policy — quality improvement becomes a condition of the relationship, not a one-time complaint.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a SCAR and how is it different from a one-off rework?

A Supplier Corrective Action Request (SCAR) is a formal, documented request that the factory find the root cause of a defect and change its process so it cannot recur. A one-off rework only fixes the units in front of you. Rework fixes this shipment; a SCAR fixes the factory. If the same defect appears in 2 or 3 batches, you need a SCAR, not another rework.

2. How fast should a Chinese factory respond to a SCAR?

A workable timeline is: acknowledgement within 24 hours, containment within 48 hours, root cause and action plan within 7 days, action completion within 30 days, and effectiveness verification over the next 3 batches (typically 30–60 days). Write the deadline into the PO terms: "SCAR close-out within 30 days or the balance is withheld."

3. What are the must-have fields in a SCAR template?

Eleven fields: SCAR number; issue date; supplier and contact; part/SKU and drawing revision; problem statement with defect quantity and detection point; immediate containment; root cause; corrective action; preventive action; owner and due date per action; and verification method plus close-out sign-off. A SCAR missing the preventive action or the owner field will not stop the defect from returning.

4. How do I tell a real root cause from an evasive reply?

A real root cause is systemic and testable; it points to a process, material spec, tool or measurement gap. Red flags of evasion: blaming the worker ("operator carelessness"), blaming the buyer ("your spec is unclear"), "we will strengthen inspection" as the only action, no preventive action, no owner and no date, and no measurement to prove the fix works.

5. Does a SCAR cost money to run?

The documentation is free but the verification costs time. A typical verification inspection is $80–$300 per man-day for 3 batches, or roughly $240–$900 total. The payoff is large: cutting a recurring 4.2% defect rate to 0.6% on a $60,000 annual spend removes about $2,160 in defects plus returns, far more than the verification cost.

6. When should I escalate from SCAR to switching suppliers?

Escalate when a factory misses the SCAR deadlines twice, cannot provide a verifiable root cause, or the defect recurs after a closed SCAR. Give one written improvement plan with a 30–60 day window and a pilot PO; if the defect rate does not fall to target, move the volume to an approved backup supplier and down-tier the factory in your scorecard.

Conclusion

A SCAR is the difference between fixing a shipment and fixing a factory. Write a precise problem statement, contain within 48 hours, drive the root cause past "operator error" with 5 Whys or a fishbone, and always demand a preventive action with an owner and a date. Verify across 3 batches before you close it. A defect that recurred at 4.2% was driven to 0.6% not by another rework but by a documented process change and a verified close-out — and that same record then raised the supplier a tier.
