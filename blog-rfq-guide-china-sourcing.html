Sourcing Strategy

How to Write an RFQ That Gets You the Best China Factory Price

Sep 17 2026 Neil Liu 12 min read

Key Takeaways

  • A well-structured RFQ gets 3–5 competitive quotes in 48–72 hours; a vague "how much for 500 units?" gets ignored or overpriced. Factories judge buyers by the quality of their inquiry — your RFQ is your first impression.
  • Include 9 essential elements: product specs, MOQ, target price, certifications, packaging, shipping terms, lead time, payment terms, and reference images. Missing any one of these triggers assumptions that cost you money.
  • Send your RFQ to 3–5 pre-screened factories — not 20+. More quotes create management overhead and decision paralysis without meaningfully improving price discovery.
  • Always request EXW and FOB pricing side-by-side to reveal hidden inland logistics costs. A factory quoting EXW $1.20 may end up more expensive than one quoting FOB $1.40 once trucking, customs, and port handling are added.
  • Real case: a US Amazon FBA buyer saved $4,200 on a 5,000-unit silicone kitchen tool order by adding 3 lines to their RFQ — target price range, FDA test report requirement, and FOB Ningbo shipping terms.

What Is an RFQ and Why It Matters in China Sourcing

An RFQ (Request for Quote) in the China sourcing context is a structured document sent to one or more Chinese factories requesting pricing, MOQ (Minimum Order Quantity), lead time, and commercial terms for a specific product. A professional RFQ is not a casual message — it is a buyer's spec sheet disguised as a price inquiry. It tells the factory: "I know what I want, I know what fair pricing looks like, and I am ready to place an order if your quote meets my requirements."

Here's the reality most first-time importers miss: a mid-sized Chinese factory receives 20–60 RFQs per day from overseas buyers. Many of these are one-liners like "How much for 500 silicone spatulas?" or "Send me price list for wireless earbuds." Sales reps triage these messages — vague inquiries get a generic catalog response or are ignored entirely. Well-structured RFQs get personal attention, fast turnarounds, and serious pricing.

Industry data from sourcing agent networks consistently shows that well-written RFQs receive 3× more quote responses than vague inquiries, and the pricing returned is typically 15–25% better. Why? Because a detailed RFQ signals to the factory that you are a real buyer, not a price-tire-kicker. Factories invest their best pricing in buyers they believe will actually place orders — and the RFQ is your evidence of seriousness.

Why Most Buyer RFQs Fail

Before we get to what a good RFQ looks like, let's identify the failure patterns. Here are the most common mistakes buyers make when contacting Chinese factories:

  • Too vague: "I want cheap shirts" or "Quote me 1,000 phones." No specs, no photos, no use case. The factory has no idea what tier of product you want.
  • Missing technical specs: No dimensions, materials, weight, or color references. The factory cannot quote accurately, so they quote high to cover risk.
  • No target price: Without an anchor, factories either inflate pricing to test your ceiling or quote their standard list price — which is rarely competitive.
  • No photos or drawings: A picture is worth a thousand words and a thousand dollars. Verbal product descriptions cause factories to imagine the wrong product entirely.
  • Unrealistic MOQ: Asking for 100 units of a custom product when the factory's MOQ is 5,000. The quote either comes back absurdly high or never comes back at all.
  • Ignoring Incoterms: Asking for "price" without specifying EXW, FOB, CIF, or DDP. You cannot compare quotes from different factories if they are quoted on different shipping bases.

The 9 Essential Elements of a High-Response RFQ

A high-response RFQ answers every question a factory's sales engineer needs to ask their production team before they can quote. Skip any one, and you trigger internal guesswork that gets priced into your quote as risk premium. Here are the 9 non-negotiable elements:

1. Product Specifications

Provide exact dimensions (with tolerances), materials, weight, color (Pantone or RAL reference when possible), finish, and any functional requirements. For multi-component products, include a bill of materials. The factory's engineering team needs this to calculate raw material cost, tooling needs, and production complexity.

  • Dimensions: length × width × height (e.g., 210mm × 60mm × 12mm, ±2mm tolerance)
  • Material grade (e.g., food-grade LFGB silicone vs. industrial silicone — 30% price difference)
  • Weight (affects raw material cost and shipping): e.g., 95g ±5g per unit
  • Colors with Pantone/RAL codes (vague "blue" means 12 different quotes)
  • Finish: matte, gloss, textured, anodized, powder-coated

2. Target MOQ (and Flexibility Range)

State your order quantity AND indicate flexibility. Factories offer tiered pricing — quote 1,000 / 3,000 / 5,000 / 10,000 unit tiers so you can see the volume break. Also state your absolute minimum (for first trial order) and your target ongoing quantity (for repeat orders). If your MOQ is below the factory's standard, acknowledge it and ask for the surcharge rather than pretending you'll order more.

3. Target Price Range (with Currency and Incoterms)

This is the single most underused RFQ element. Buyers fear that revealing a target price lets the factory "anchor" their quote to it. The opposite is true: providing a realistic target range tells the factory you've done your market research and saves 2–3 negotiation rounds. Rule of thumb: set your target 10–15% below your actual ceiling — never reveal your true maximum. Always specify currency (USD preferred) and Incoterms (e.g., "USD 1.40–1.65 FOB Ningbo").

4. Required Certifications

List every regulatory certification your destination market requires. This is non-negotiable for legal import. Common examples:

  • USA: FCC (electronics), FDA / LFGB (food contact), CPC (children's products), UL (electrical safety), Prop 65 (California)
  • EU: CE mark, RoHS, REACH, EN71 (toys), LFGB (food contact)
  • UK: UKCA mark, UK toy safety regulations
  • Australia: RCMM, AS/NZS standards
  • Middle East: SASO (Saudi), ECAS (UAE), G-Mark (GCC)

Request that the factory include test reports from accredited labs (SGS, TÜV, Intertek) in their quote. A factory that cannot produce certification documents should be deprioritized, regardless of price.

5. Packaging Requirements

Packaging affects landed cost more than buyers realize. Specify:

  • Retail packaging: custom printed box, polybag, blister card, or bulk
  • Inner pack: units per inner carton, polybag with header card or zip-lock
  • Master carton: dimensions, units per carton, gross weight
  • Labeling: barcode (UPC/EAN), FNSKU for Amazon FBA, country-of-origin mark, multilingual labels
  • FBA prep: ASIN labels, suffocation warning on polybags, bubble-wrap fragile items
  • Shipping marks: per carton, per pallet — including PO number, item code, quantity

6. Shipping Terms (EXW, FOB, CIF, DDP — Request at Least Two)

Incoterms determine who bears which logistics costs. Always request at least two — typically EXW (Ex Works) and FOB (Free On Board) — so you can see the inland logistics cost component:

  • EXW: Factory price only — you arrange and pay for everything from the factory door onward. Useful for benchmarking pure production cost.
  • FOB: Factory delivers goods to the origin port (e.g., FOB Ningbo, FOB Shenzhen) and clears export customs. Most common for sea freight.
  • CIF: Cost, Insurance, Freight to destination port. Convenient but obscures the actual shipping cost — factories often mark this up 10–20%.
  • DDP: Delivered Duty Paid to your warehouse — door-to-door with all duties and taxes included. Simplest for the buyer; preferred for first-time importers and air freight small orders.

7. Lead Time Requirements

State your target delivery date and your flexibility window. Distinguish between production lead time (factory's time to manufacture — typically 25–45 days) and shipping lead time (sea freight: 18–40 days depending on route; air freight: 5–10 days). Also specify whether you need container loading supervision or pre-shipment inspection — these add 2–4 days but protect the order.

8. Payment Terms

State your acceptable payment structure upfront. The China standard is 30% T/T deposit on order confirmation, 70% balance after pre-shipment inspection pass and before container loading. For new relationships, expect to pay this way; for established relationships, you can negotiate to L/C (Letter of Credit) at sight, OA (Open Account) 30/60/90 days, or escrow via trade assurance. State your terms in the RFQ — factories will quote differently based on payment risk.

9. Reference Photos or Technical Drawings

The single highest-impact element of your RFQ. Attach:

  • 3–5 reference photos of a similar product (yours or a competitor's)
  • Technical drawing (PDF or DWG) with dimensions and tolerances
  • Approved golden sample photo if you have one
  • Photos showing critical features: joints, finishes, packaging

Without visuals, three different factories will imagine three different products and quote three prices that aren't comparable. With visuals, you get apples-to-apples quotes within 48 hours.

How to Compare Multiple Factory Quotes

Once your RFQ returns 3–5 quotes, you need a structured comparison — not a price-rank-and-pick. The lowest unit price is rarely the lowest total cost. Use this comparison table template:

For a first trial order, Factory C is the better choice despite the higher unit price — the total cash exposure is half, and faster delivery lets you start selling sooner. Always calculate total landed cost, not just unit price.

How to calculate real cost difference: Effective FOB price = EXW price + inland trucking + port handling + export customs clearance. Total landed cost = FOB price + sea freight/unit + destination port charges + import duty + customs broker fee. A factory quoting $0.20 cheaper on EXW but located 800km inland can end up $0.30 more expensive on FOB after trucking costs. Geography matters — coastal factories near Ningbo, Shenzhen, or Shanghai typically offer the lowest FOB prices.

Red Flags in Factory Quotes

Watch for these warning signs when reviewing returned quotes:

  • Price too good to be true: 30–40% below the average quote almost always means material substitution, counterfeit components, or a bait-and-switch on the sample. Walk away.
  • No MOQ flexibility: A factory refusing to budge from a 10,000-unit MOQ for a new buyer signals either low confidence in repeat business or a trading company posing as a factory.
  • Refuses to provide samples: If a factory won't ship a sample (even paid), they cannot be trusted with a 5,000-unit order. Always require a paid sample before placing a bulk order.
  • No export license: Some small workshops can manufacture but cannot legally export. They will route your goods through a third-party trading company, adding 5–10% markup and obscuring responsibility for defects.
  • Pushes 100% upfront payment: Standard terms are 30/70. A factory demanding 100% before production is either desperate for cash or planning to skip quality commitments.
  • Vague lead time ("soon" / "fast"): A serious factory gives a specific day count (e.g., 35 days). "Soon" means they don't actually know their production schedule.
  • No response to certification request: If they can't produce FDA/FCC/CE documents within 48 hours, they don't have them — and the certifications may not exist.
  • Quote valid for 90+ days: Material prices fluctuate. A factory quoting a fixed price for 90 days is either overpricing to hedge or planning to renegotiate later.

Real Case Study: How 3 Extra Lines Saved $4,200

A US-based Amazon FBA seller sourcing 5,000 silicone kitchen tool sets (spatula, spoon, whisk, tongs, brush — 5-piece set) initially sent this RFQ to four factories through a Yiwu trading contact:

Original RFQ (5 lines):
"We need 5,000 silicone kitchen tool sets. 5-piece set. Ship to USA. Please send your best price. Thanks."

Result: 2 of 4 factories responded after 4 days. Quotes returned at $2.10 and $2.25 per set EXW — both well above the buyer's target. The trading contact explained: "The factories think you want premium retail-grade silicone with custom packaging. Without specs, they're quoting the high end to cover risk."

The buyer rewrote the RFQ with the Yeatru template, adding three critical lines:

  1. Target price range: "USD 1.50–1.80 per set FOB Ningbo"
  2. Certification requirement: "FDA test report from SGS/TÜV/Intertek required — include in quote"
  3. Material options: "Please quote 3 options: (a) food-grade silicone + stainless-steel core; (b) food-grade silicone only; (c) platinum-cured silicone"

Same four factories, plus two additional verified silicone specialists sourced through the Yeatru network, returned quotes within 48 hours:

  • Factory 1: $1.65/set (FDA certified, premium silicone)
  • Factory 2: $1.52/set (FDA certified, standard food-grade silicone)
  • Factory 3: $1.45/set (FDA certified, standard food-grade silicone + stainless-steel core) — selected
  • Factory 4: $1.38/set (FDA test report pending — too risky)

Outcome: Buyer selected Factory 3 at $1.45/set FOB Ningbo with FDA test report included. The original $2.10 quote (no target, no cert requirement) would have cost $10,500. The improved RFQ landed the order at $7,250 — a savings of $3,250 on unit price plus an additional $950 saved by avoiding the buyer's backup plan (a US-based 3PL repack at $0.19/unit to add missing FDA labels). Total savings: $4,200 on a single 5,000-unit order — directly attributable to three added lines in the RFQ.

The buyer is now a recurring Yeatru client sourcing 15,000–20,000 silicone kitchen sets per quarter. Estimated annual savings from the structured RFQ process: $12,000–$18,000 in unit pricing alone.
