Product Compliance Inspection for EU/US Market Access

CE, FCC, FDA, CPC, RoHS — compliance checks before shipping.

Key Takeaways

  • A compliance inspection is a pre-shipment document & marking audit, separate from a quality (AQL) inspection. It verifies that the product and its paperwork meet the destination market's legal requirements.
  • Never trust a factory's "we have CE/FCC" claim. Verify every certificate number on the issuing body's website — fake and expired certificates are common.
  • A certificate only covers the exact tested model. A changed battery, plastic grade, or even color may invalidate it. Confirm the model/materials match the shipped product.
  • Marking matters as much as the certificate. A product with a valid CE test report but no CE mark (or a CE mark under 5mm) is still non-compliant in the EU.
  • Do compliance checks twice: before placing the order (certificates valid?) and before shipment (marking & mass-production samples match?). See product certifications and food contact materials.

What Is a Product Compliance Inspection?

A product compliance inspection confirms that the product you are about to import meets the legal requirements of the market where it will be sold. It has three parts: (1) document audit — verify the Declaration of Conformity, test reports, and certificates; (2) marking & labeling check — confirm the required marks (CE, FCC ID, RoHS, WEEE) are present, correct, and legible; and (3) sample verification — for regulated products, test a mass-production sample against the certificate. It is distinct from a quality inspection (which checks defects), but both are typically done before the final balance payment. A compliance failure means the product cannot legally be sold — and in the EU, the importer is legally responsible.

Step 1: List the Required Certifications

Before anything, map your product and destination market to the required certifications. For example, a Bluetooth speaker sold in the EU needs CE (RED) + RoHS + WEEE; sold in the US needs FCC Certification (FCC ID) + state RoHS. A children's plastic toy sold in the EU needs CE (EN71) + REACH; sold in the US needs CPSC + CPSIA + CPC. If you skip this step, you will not know which documents to request. See product certifications from China for the full category-to-certification table.

Step 2: Collect All Compliance Documents

Ask the factory for the full compliance package before production (or at the latest, before the balance payment):

  • Declaration of Conformity (DoC / DoC) — the legal document stating the product meets the relevant directives.
  • Test reports — CE LVD/EMC/RED, FCC grant, RoHS, EN71/CPSIA, FDA (as applicable).
  • Technical file index — list of the documents supporting the DoC (EU requirement).
  • Material / BOM list — to confirm the materials match the test report.

Step 3: Verify Each Certificate Online

This is the most important step. Fake and expired certificates are common. Verify independently:

  • CE test reports: confirm the lab is ISO 17025 accredited and enter the report number on the lab's verification portal. If a Notified Body is involved, check the NB number on the EU NANDO database.
  • FCC ID: enter the FCC ID into the FCC ID database (fccid.io or apps.fcc.gov). The grant should show the correct product and grantee.
  • RoHS / EN71 / CPSIA reports: verify the lab report number on the issuing lab's website.
  • FDA: confirm the facility registration number in the FDA database (for food, drugs, medical devices).

Step 4: Confirm Coverage of Your Exact Product

A certificate for model BT-100 does not cover model BT-200. A certificate for a lithium-ion battery from Supplier A does not cover a battery from Supplier B. Check that the model number, materials, and components on the certificate match the product being shipped. If anything changed — even the color, the plastic grade, or the battery supplier — you need a new test or a difference assessment from the lab. This is the #1 compliance failure: buyers ship a modified product under an unmodified certificate.

Step 5: Inspect Product Marking & Labeling

A valid test report is useless if the product marking is wrong. Check on the finished product:

  • EU: CE mark (minimum 5mm high, proportional, not distorted), importer name & address, serial or batch number, country of origin ("Made in China"), and for electronics the WEEE crossed-out-bin symbol.
  • US: FCC ID (for intentional radiators) or FCC compliance statement (SDOC), and for children's products a permanent tracking label + CPC reference.
  • Marking must be durable and legible — printed or engraved, not a sticker that falls off.

Step 6: Pre-Shipment Document & Sample Audit

Before paying the balance and releasing the shipment:

  1. Audit 1–3 mass-production samples against the test report (materials, markings, model number).
  2. For regulated products (toys, food-contact materials, medical devices, children's products), send a mass-production sample to a third-party lab for a verification test. A pre-production sample that passed is worthless if the factory switched materials during mass production.
  3. Photograph the marking on at least 10 samples to confirm consistency.

Step 7: File Records & Release Shipment

Only release the balance payment and shipment after all documents and marking pass the audit. File the DoC, test reports, marking photos, and sample test results for 10 years in the EU (and as required by US agencies). In the EU, market surveillance authorities can request these documents at any time — if you cannot produce them, the product is presumed non-compliant and may be recalled.

Worked Example: CE Compliance Inspection for a USB Charger

A buyer ordered 8,000 USB-C chargers at $2.10/unit EXW for the EU market. The factory provided a CE DoC and an EMC test report for model UC-500. During the pre-shipment compliance inspection:

  • Certificate check: the EMC report from SGS was valid and verified online. ✓
  • Model match: the shipped product was model UC-500. ✓
  • LVD gap: the factory only had an EMC test report, not an LVD (electrical safety) test report. The DoC claimed LVD compliance but had no supporting test. ✗ — non-compliant.
  • Marking: the CE mark was only 3mm high (below the 5mm minimum). ✗ — non-compliant.

Result: the buyer held the shipment, required the factory to add an LVD test ($1,200, 2 weeks) and reprint the CE mark at 5mm. The delay cost 14 days but avoided a potential product recall and market-surveillance fine. The LVD test was a one-time cost of $0.15/unit spread over 8,000 units.

Common Red Flags

  • Factory refuses to share the actual certificate — only says "we have CE."
  • Certificate from an unknown lab with no online verification portal.
  • Certificate expired or issued more than 5 years ago with no retest.
  • Model number on the certificate differs from the product.
  • CE mark missing, distorted, or under 5mm.
  • FCC ID not found in the FCC ID database.
  • DoC signed by someone without authority to represent the manufacturer.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a product compliance inspection?

A product compliance inspection is a pre-shipment check that the product and its documentation meet the legal requirements of the destination market. It covers certificate verification (CE, FCC, RoHS, FDA, CPSC), correct product marking and labeling, and a document audit. It is separate from a quality (AQL) inspection but is often done together.

2. How do I verify a CE certificate from China?

1) Get the Declaration of Conformity (DoC) and the test report. 2) Confirm the test lab is ISO 17025 accredited. 3) Enter the report number on the lab's verification portal. 4) Check the certificate is in date. 5) Confirm the model number, materials, and components on the certificate match the product being shipped. If a Notified Body is involved, verify the NB number on the EU NANDO database.

3. How do I verify an FCC ID?

Go to the FCC ID database (fccid.io or apps.fcc.gov) and enter the FCC ID printed on the product. The grant should show the correct product, grantee, and test reports. If the FCC ID doesn't exist or belongs to a different product, the marking is invalid and the product cannot be sold in the US.

4. Can a fake certificate be detected?

Yes. Fake certificates usually fail one or more checks: the lab is unknown or not accredited, the report number doesn't verify on the lab's website, the certificate is expired, the model number doesn't match, or the document looks scanned/edited. Always verify independently rather than trusting the factory's copy.

5. What marking must be on the product?

For the EU: CE mark (min 5mm, proportional), importer name/address, serial or batch number, country of origin, and for electronics the WEEE crossed-out bin. For the US: FCC ID (intentional radiators) or FCC statement (SDOC), and for children's products a tracking label and CPC reference. Marking must be durable and legible.

6. When should I do a compliance inspection?

Do a compliance check before placing the order (verify the factory holds valid certificates) and again before shipment (audit mass-production samples and marking against the certificates). For regulated products (toys, food-contact, medical), add a third-party verification test on mass-production samples before release.

Conclusion

A product compliance inspection verifies that your China-sourced product can legally be sold in the destination market. It is a 7-step process: list required certifications, collect documents, verify each certificate online, confirm coverage of your exact product, inspect marking and labeling, audit mass-production samples, and file records. The most common failures are expired/fake certificates, model-number mismatches, and missing or undersized CE marks. A $0.15/unit LVD test and a 5mm CE mark are cheap insurance against a product recall. Ask Yeatru to run a compliance inspection on your next order before shipment.
